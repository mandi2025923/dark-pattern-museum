WHY THIS EXISTS

Every interface you use is designed to steer you. This museum exists to make that steering visible.

Dark Pattern Museum is a postgraduate final major project for the MA Digital Media and Creative Industries at the University of Birmingham. It does not solve dark patterns. It restages them so you can see how they work — and ask why they are everywhere.

Theoretical framework

Critique through interaction design — not walls of academic text

Dark pattern theory

Interfaces that trick users into doing things they didn't mean to do. From Brignull's original 2010 taxonomy to Gray et al.'s 2024 CHI ontology of 64 types — a decade and a half of naming what platforms would rather keep invisible.

→ Gray et al. (2024) — CHI ontology

Attention economy

Your attention is the product. Platforms optimise for time-on-site because more scrolling means more ads served. Every notification, auto-play, and infinite feed is infrastructure for extracting attention at scale.

→ Tim Wu — The Attention Merchants (2017)

Persuasive technology

Computers don't just respond to behaviour — they shape it. Fogg's (2003) framework showed how digital products systematically guide what users do, feel, and decide. The same tools that help you build habits can also exploit them.

→ Fogg (2003) — Persuasive Technology

Platform capitalism

A few companies own the infrastructure where digital life happens. They set the rules, control the data, and design the interfaces. When the platform itself is the product, user autonomy is a cost to be managed.

→ Srnicek (2017) — Platform Capitalism

Further reading

Key regulatory and research resources on dark patterns

  • FTC — Bringing Dark Patterns to Light (2022)The US Federal Trade Commission's landmark report on dark patterns in digital commerce, outlining enforcement priorities under Section 5 of the FTC Act.
  • FTC Act Section 5 — Unfair or Deceptive Acts or PracticesThe primary US legal instrument used to regulate deceptive interface design, including dark patterns that cause consumer harm.
  • EU Digital Services Act — Article 25Prohibits online platform interfaces that deceive or manipulate users, including designs that impair informed decision-making.
  • EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)EU regulation governing data protection and privacy, directly relevant to dark patterns in cookie consent and data-sharing interfaces.
  • Gray et al. (2024) — An Ontology of Dark Patterns (CHI 2024)The foundational academic taxonomy unifying 64 dark pattern types across prior classification systems.
  • Brignull — Deceptive Design (Dark Patterns)Harry Brignull's original taxonomy of deceptive design patterns, continuously updated with real-world examples since 2010.
